Cash or Stay Invested?

A forecast and scenario plan for U.S. equities through late 2029, built around the four forces that will decide it: AI capital spending, interest rates, inflation, and the labor market. Prepared for Nathan Hale's advisory board.

Executive summary

Recommendation A confidence 70%

Stay invested in the long-term core. Raise cash deliberately, not defensively: enough short-term Treasuries and money-market to cover 24 months of planned withdrawals plus a 10% reserve, funded by trimming the most AI-concentrated holdings, inside tax-advantaged accounts first.

The next twelve months look like a coin flip between stocks and cash. Our probability-weighted S&P 500 path lands near today's level a year out, while T-bills pay about 4%. Over 36 months the weighting still favors equities by roughly 10 percentage points cumulatively, and the historical odds of a positive three-year return sit near 84%. So the answer is sizing, not timing: cash covers the near-term need and the bear case, equities carry the long-term job.

What the evidence says, in six lines

  1. Valuations are stretched but earnings are real. Forward P/E of 18.7 is near its five-year average after a 36% earnings surge in 2026, yet the Shiller CAPE near 40 is second only to 1999, and the equity risk premium over the 10-year Treasury is under half a point.
  2. The Fed has turned. Chair Kevin Warsh's Fed hiked in September 2026, held at 3.75%–4.00% in October with two dissents for another hike, and markets price a December hike at roughly 80%. The dot plot shows no cuts in 2027.
  3. Inflation re-accelerated on oil. Headline CPI is 3.7% after the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz and pushed Brent above $100. Core is 2.6%. Peace talks are the swing factor.
  4. Growth is strong but narrow. Q3 GDP grew 3.4%, much of it AI equipment spending, while September payrolls rose only 29,000 and unemployment sits at 4.2%. Fidelity still calls the U.S. mid-cycle; the CNBC Fed Survey puts recession odds at 26%.
  5. AI is the hinge. Big Tech will spend about $735 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 and analysts pencil in $0.9 to $1.3 trillion for 2027. Alphabet lost 12.6% in a day when it guided capex "significantly" higher. Credit, not chip demand, is showing strain first: neocloud bonds yield near 15%. Elon Musk says the bottleneck is now power, not chips, and expects neocloud consolidation and a 2027 slowdown in data-center investment.
  6. Politics adds friction, not direction. Democrats won the Senate and, per the networks, the House. Expect gridlock, debt-ceiling brinkmanship, and tech investigations. Goldman sees a possible trim to data-center tax incentives but no moratorium.

The four forces

1. AI: the capex supercycle meets its financing test

The 2026 bull market is an earnings story first. S&P 500 profits are on track to grow about 36% this year, with 2027 consensus near 16%. The hyperscalers built that growth with cash flow, and that is now the problem: Alphabet spent $51 billion on capex against $42 billion of operating cash flow in Q3 and posted negative free cash flow. Nvidia is discussing a $250 billion backstop for OpenAI's Ohio campus. Five tech companies accounted for almost 10% of global investment-grade bond issuance in 2026. The IMF and the Bank of England both flag financing, not demand, as the weak link.

The bull case is still intact on the demand side. Nvidia's July quarter revenue was $96 billion, up 106%, and it reports again on November 17. Oracle rallied 10.7% the day Alphabet fell, because enterprise cloud demand accelerated. Jason Furman notes U.S. productivity growth since 2022 has run about twice its pre-pandemic pace. Vanguard's framing is the one to hold: "economic upside, stock market downside," meaning AI can lift GDP while index returns disappoint because the price was paid up front.

2. Interest rates: higher for longer became higher again

The easing cycle that began in September 2025 ended at 3.50%–3.75% in December 2025. Kevin Warsh took the chair in May 2026 as an inflation hawk, and the September 2026 hike was the first since 2023. The dot plot's median sits at 4.1% for both end-2026 and end-2027, with 3.9% in 2028. The 10-year yield crossed 5% in October on the hawkish hold and heavy Treasury supply, and Ken Rogoff told Bloomberg in August that the fiscal problem will not be addressed until a shock forces it. For stocks the arithmetic is simple: an 18.7 forward multiple gives a 5.3% earnings yield against a 4.9% risk-free rate. Equities are priced for everything to go right.

3. Inflation: oil did what tariffs could not

The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026, and the New York Fed estimates tariff-driven goods inflation faded from about 3% to 2% by August. Then the Iran war closed Hormuz. Gasoline rose 9.1% in September alone and lifted headline CPI to 3.7%. Core at 2.6% says the underlying trend is tolerable; the Fed's own projection has core PCE at 2.5% in 2027. The October CPI prints on November 10. If Brent holds above $100 into 2027, the December hike becomes a sequence and the bear case gains weight. If a peace deal reopens the strait, headline inflation could drop a full point within two quarters and the Fed's hiking bias fades.

4. Unemployment and growth: strong GDP, weak hiring

This is the oddest pairing in the data. Output grew 3.4% in Q3 on AI equipment and a 3.8% rise in consumer spending, yet payroll growth has slowed to a crawl and job postings only turned up in September. Unemployment at 4.2% is below the 4.4% of a year ago, so the labor market is cooling without cracking. Fidelity's Q4 business cycle update describes "sturdy mid-cycle dynamics." Polymarket prices a 32% chance of recession by end-2027; the CNBC Fed Survey says 26% within a year. A recession is the minority case, but it is the case in which an AI capex pause and consumer fatigue arrive together.

The long-horizon argument for staying

Every bear case above is a 12-to-24-month story. The reason to keep the core invested is that the odds flip with time: since 1926 the S&P 500 has been positive in about three of every four single years, 84% of three-year spans, and 94% of ten-year spans, including the years that began at CAPE readings like today's. High starting valuations lower the expected return (Vanguard's 4%–6% a year is the honest number) but they do not make the return negative over a decade. Cash at 4% today is a real return of only about 0.3% after 3.7% inflation, and that yield falls the moment the Fed cuts, which is exactly what happens in the bear case.

The advisory board weighs in

Five seats, one question each. The lenses are the ones Nathan set in April 2026: himself as principal investor, his wife as the teacher, the kids as long-term shareholders, Drew on investments, Ian on tax.

Nathan

Principal investor · CRE capital-markets lens

You have watched "extend and pretend" play out in commercial real estate since 2022, and the AI data-center build has the same shape: long leases, floating-rate construction debt, and tenants whose revenue has not yet arrived. The $1.65 trillion of off-balance-sheet lease obligations at five companies is the number a CRE underwriter would circle first.

Verdict: treat AI exposure like a single-tenant asset. Cap it, do not exit it.

The teacher

Household stability · the sleep-at-night test

The question she will ask is not "what is the S&P doing" but "can we cover the next two years without selling anything in a panic." A 24-month reserve in T-bills at 4% answers that regardless of which scenario arrives, and it removes the one decision that destroys returns, which is selling after a 20% drop.

Verdict: fund the reserve now while yields are still above inflation.

The kids

Long-term shareholders · 30-year horizon

Their accounts have no 36-month problem. A 94% ten-year hit rate and a 100% twenty-year hit rate say their money stays fully invested, ideally in a broad index that owns whatever replaces today's leaders. A 2027 drawdown is a buying opportunity for them, not a threat.

Verdict: no change. Keep contributing, especially into a selloff.

Drew

Investment advisor · allocation and rebalancing

The portfolio has drifted toward the Magnificent Seven simply by holding an index for three years. Rebalance back to target, add a value and dividend tilt (Vanguard's highest-ranked U.S. sleeve), and take the international weight up: emerging markets were up 23% through October against 14% for the S&P. Intermediate Treasuries at 4.5%–5% finally do their job as ballast again.

Verdict: rebalance, diversify, and set rules so the next move is mechanical.

Ian

Tax advisor · taxable vs. tax-advantaged

Trim concentrated positions inside the IRA and 401(k) first, where there is no tax bill. In taxable accounts, harvest losses in AI names that have fallen 20%–30% from their peaks to offset gains elsewhere, and sell only lots held over a year. Keep the T-bill reserve in taxable accounts, since Treasury interest is exempt from state tax, and keep the bond ballast in the IRA.

Verdict: the recommendation costs almost nothing in tax if sequenced this way.

Probability this beats Course B over 36 months: about 30%.

Rules that make the next move mechanical

  • Reserve first. Move the 24-month withdrawal need plus a 10% reserve into a 3-to-12-month T-bill ladder and a government money-market fund over the next four weeks, one quarter each week, so a single day's price does not decide it.
  • Trim by rule, not by mood. Any single stock above 8% of the portfolio or any sector above 35% gets trimmed back on the next rebalance date. The Magnificent Seven are near a third of the S&P 500 by weight.
  • Rebalance quarterly, or at a 5-point drift. A 20% drawdown in the bear case automatically turns the reserve into a buyer at lower prices.
  • Buy the bear case in thirds. If the index falls 15%, deploy a third of the 10% reserve; another third at 25%; the last third at 30% or when the Fed's first cut arrives, whichever is first.
  • Do nothing on headlines. The midterm result, the Tesla and SpaceX merger talk, and the next Musk forecast are not triggers. Only the tripwires below are.
